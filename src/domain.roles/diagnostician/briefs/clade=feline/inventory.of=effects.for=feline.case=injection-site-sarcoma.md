# inventory.of=effects.for=feline.case=injection-site-sarcoma

> ⚠️ **not veterinary advice — informational only.** this brief is research groundwork compiled
> from public sources to the best of our knowledge; it carries **no guarantee of outcome** and is
> **not a substitute for a licensed veterinarian.** a lump that meets the "3-2-1" criteria below
> needs a vet's biopsy — do not wait it out on your own read.

## .what

**feline injection-site sarcoma (FISS)** — historically "vaccine-associated sarcoma" — is a rare
but serious **malignant tumour** that can arise **at the site of any injection** (vaccine or other
injectable) in a cat. it is the **most severe** of the post-vaccinal effects. it is **rare**, it
develops **months to years** after the shot, and it is **not the same** as the normal sore,
short-lived lump most cats get for a couple weeks (that ordinary reaction is covered in
`inventory.of=effects.for=feline.case=postvaccinal-reaction.md`).

this brief is the **zoom-in** on that one rare effect: what it is, why it forms, how likely, and
how it is watched for.

## .where it sits in the causal chain

FISS is an **`effect:mechanism`** (a lesion / pathological state), reached from the
**`cause:exposure`** (the injection) via the **`cause:mechanism`** (chronic local inflammation).
see `../define.causal-chain-frame.[lesson].md`.

```
cause:exposure ──▶ cause:mechanism ─────────▶ effect:mechanism ──▶ effect:symptom
  any injection     chronic inflammation        FISS (sarcoma)       a firm, persistent,
  (vaccine, etc.)   at the site                                      enlarged lump
```

## .why it forms — chronic inflammation, then transformation

the leading hypothesis across the literature is consistent: the local inflammation an injection
provokes, if it becomes **chronic**, can trigger malignant transformation of the repair cells
(fibroblasts).

From [Hartmann et al. 2015, ABCD guideline (*J Feline Med Surg*), PMC11148925](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26101312):
> "chronic inflammatory reactions are considered the trigger for subsequent malignant transformation. ... Adjuvanted vaccines induce intense local inflammation and seem therefore to be particularly linked to the development of FISS. The risk is lower for modified-live and recombinant vaccines, but no vaccine is risk-free."

From [Lu et al. 2023 (*J Cell Mol Med*), PMC10399534](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37334757):
> "there is a consensus that FISS is associated with chronic inflammation caused by irritation of injection-related trauma and foreign chemical substances."

From [Shih et al. 2022 (*BMC Vet Res*), PMC9281114](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35836213):
> "The pathogenesis of FISS has been correlated with chronic inflammation, resulting in neoplastic transformation."

From [Santelices Iglesias et al. 2018 (*J Comp Pathol*)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30502795):
> "Vaccine adjuvants can trigger an intense and persistent inflammatory response that may lead to neoplastic transformation."

the molecular pathways implicated are the same ones that drive inflammation-linked cancers —
adjuvant aluminium, NF-κB, COX-2, STAT3:

From [Hsueh et al. 2019 (*BMC Vet Res*), PMC6815016](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31653220):
> "The disease has been considered to be associated with vaccine adjuvant, aluminum, which serves as a stimulus continuously inducing overzealous inflammatory and immunologic reactions."

From [Wei et al. 2019 (*BMC Cancer*), PMC6449919](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30947707):
> "Feline injection-site sarcoma (FISS), an aggressive iatrogenic subcutaneous malignancy, is challenging to manage clinically".

> **the honest caveat** — the inflammation→sarcoma link is a hypothesis, not proven cause. From
> [Kass 2017 (*Vet Clin North Am*)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29217316):
> > "It is scientifically untenable to address FISS risk based on propensity of vaccines to elicit differential inflammatory responses if none of those responses led to sarcoma development. ... such guidelines must be regarded with skepticism."

## .how likely — rare

| figure | value | source |
|--------|-------|--------|
| UK estimate | 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000 vaccinations | Kliczkowska 2015 |
| general practice (Poland) | ~16 per 10,000 cats | Kliczkowska 2015 |
| US reaction vs sarcoma | 11.8 inflammatory reactions vs **0.3 FISS** per 10,000 vaccines | ABCD guideline |
| owner-facing estimate | "less than 1 in 20,000 vaccines given" | iCatCare |

From [Kliczkowska et al. 2015 (*Pol J Vet Sci*)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26172181):
> "The prevalence is estimated between 1 in 1000 and 1 in 10 000 vaccinations in the United Kingdom."

From [International Cat Care](https://icatcare.org/articles/vaccinating-your-cat):
> "In very rare cases (around less than 1 in 20,000 vaccines given), a feline injection site sarcoma (FISS) could develop".

## .how it behaves — aggressive, locally invasive

From the [ABCD HTML guideline](https://www.abcdcatsvets.org/guideline-for-feline-injection-site-sarcoma/):
> "invasive sarcomas (mostly fibrosarcomas) ... have characteristics that are distinct from those of fibrosarcomas in other areas and behave more aggressively. The rate of metastasis ranges from 10% to 28%."

it favors the classic injection sites — the **hindlimb** among them:
> "FISS occur at sites typically used for vaccination and injections, such as the interscapular region ... and in the area of the semimembranosus and semitendinosus muscles in the hindlimbs."

and its latency is long:
> "FISS can develop as early as four months and up to two to three years after an injection".

## .the vaccines historically linked

rabies and FeLV — the classically adjuvanted vaccines — were the first implicated, though any
injection can be a cause. From the [ABCD HTML guideline](https://www.abcdcatsvets.org/guideline-for-feline-injection-site-sarcoma/):
> "This observation was connected to an increased use of rabies and feline leukaemia virus (FeLV) vaccinations ... these tumours were first called feline 'vaccine-associated sarcomas'."

modern non-adjuvanted / recombinant products aim to lower that risk. From
[Stachura et al. 2025 (*Vaccines*), PMC12737566](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41441643):
> "Although a number of vaccines exist for domestic animals, most contain inactivated rabies virus with adjuvants."

and the broader review confirms no product is fully exempt — from
[Hartmann et al. 2023 (*Viruses*), PMC10459272](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37632050):
> "No injectable vaccine is risk-free, and therefore, vaccination should be performed as often as necessary, but as infrequently as possible."

## .how it is watched for — the "3-2-1 rule"

the watch rule every source agrees on. From the [ABCD HTML guideline](https://www.abcdcatsvets.org/guideline-for-feline-injection-site-sarcoma/):
> "Any lump at the site of injection that is still present three months after vaccination, or that is larger than 2 cm in diameter, or that is increasing in size one month after vaccination should be surgically removed and investigated through histopathology."

for owners, from [AVMA](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/petcare/vaccinations):
> "It's also possible that a small, firm swelling under the skin could develop at the vaccination site soon after vaccination. This swelling should start to disappear within a couple of weeks. If it lasts more than three weeks, seems painful, or seems to be getting larger, contact your veterinarian."

and the reassurance that vaccination should not be abandoned over this rare risk — from
[Cornell — Feline Vaccines: Benefits and Risks](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-vaccines-benefits-and-risks):
> "For the average cat, the benefits of an appropriate vaccination program (protection against serious/lethal diseases) far outweigh the potential risks associated with vaccination."

## .how it is prevented and treated

**prevention** (from the [ABCD HTML guideline](https://www.abcdcatsvets.org/guideline-for-feline-injection-site-sarcoma/)): vaccinate only as often as necessary; prefer non-adjuvanted/mucosal products where proven equivalent; single-dose vials; **distal-limb** placement so a mass can be excised (or the limb amputated) with clear margins —
> "Vaccines should be injected at sites where any subsequent mass could be easily surgically removed, preferably distally in a limb."

**treatment** (from [Hartmann et al. 2015, PMC11148925](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26101312)):
> "Aggressive, radical excision is required to avoid tumour recurrence. The prognosis improves if additional radiotherapy and/or immunotherapy (such as recombinant feline IL-2) are used."

## .the takeaway for the two-thigh case

both injection sites (both thighs) should be **watched over the months ahead**, not just days. the
short-lived soreness/limp is the *normal* reaction (see the companion brief). FISS is the *rare*
one, months–years out, flagged by a **persistent lump, one over 2 cm, or one that grows** at a
site — that is the signal that turns a routine site-check into a vet visit and biopsy.
